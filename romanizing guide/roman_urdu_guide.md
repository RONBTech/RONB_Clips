# Casual Roman Urdu (Roman Urdu / Urdu-English) Typing Guide

*A practical guide to how Urdu speakers commonly type Urdu in the English/Roman alphabet in everyday messages, chats, comments and social media.*

## 1. What this guide is for

This guide focuses on **casual Roman Urdu**: Urdu written with English letters instead of the Urdu script. It reflects common informal usage, especially in Pakistani digital communication, where Roman Urdu is widely used in texts, WhatsApp messages, comments and online discussions.

Examples:

- کیا حال ہے؟ → `kya haal hai?`
- آپ کہاں ہیں؟ → `aap kahan hain?`
- مجھے نہیں معلوم۔ → `mujhe nahi maloom.`
- آپ کیسے ہیں؟ → `aap kaise hain?`
- میں گھر جا رہا ہوں۔ → `main ghar ja raha hu.`

You may also see Urdu-English mixing, often called **Urdish** or code-mixed Urdu:

- `Kal office jana hai.`
- `Mujhe ye file send kar dena.`
- `Meeting ke baad baat karte hain.`

The aim is not formal transliteration. It is to write in a way that feels natural and immediately readable to everyday Urdu speakers.

## 2. The main rule: Roman Urdu has no single standard

People spell words according to how they pronounce them, what they are used to seeing, their region, and how quickly they are typing. As a result, one Urdu word can have several common Roman spellings.

| Urdu | Common Roman Urdu variations |
|---|---|
| نہیں | `nahi`, `nahin`, `nai`, `nhi` |
| بہت | `bohat`, `bahut`, `buhat`, `boht` |
| اچھا | `acha`, `achha`, `accha` |
| کیسے | `kaise`, `kaisay`, `kese` |
| کیوں | `kyun`, `kiun`, `kyu` |
| کیا | `kya`, `kia` |
| مجھے | `mujhe`, `mujhy`, `muje` |
| زندگی | `zindagi`, `zindagee`, `zindagy` |
| پیار | `pyar`, `pyaar` |
| چاہیے | `chahiye`, `chaheye` |

These variations are part of everyday usage, not necessarily mistakes. Research on Roman Urdu messaging and online text has repeatedly documented multiple spellings for the same word and the lack of a universally followed spelling standard.

**Recommended approach:** use familiar, readable forms consistently. This guide generally prefers `nahi`, `bohat`, `acha`, `kaise`, `kyun`, `kya`, `mujhe` and `zindagi`, while recognizing common alternatives.

## 3. Vowels: simple English letters

Casual Roman Urdu uses ordinary English letters. Do not use academic marks such as ā, ī, ū, ḥ or ʿ in everyday messages.

| Sound / Urdu example | Common casual form |
|---|---|
| اَ — اب | `ab` |
| آ — آپ | `aap` |
| اِ — ادھر | `idhar` |
| ای — تین | `teen` |
| اُ — ادھر / اُٹھ | `uth` |
| او — دور | `door` |
| اے — ایسے | `aise` |
| او — لوگ | `log` |
| اور | `aur` |

### Long vowels

Writers often repeat a vowel to show a longer sound:

- `aa`: `aap`, `aaj`, `baat`, `naam`
- `ee`: `theek`, `ameer`, `jeet`
- `oo`: `door`, `dhoop`, `socho`

But there is no strict rule. `pyar` and `pyaar` are both common. Use the spelling that readers will recognize.

### Urdu sounds that casual typing often merges

Urdu has several letters that may be pronounced similarly or identically by many speakers in everyday speech. Casual Roman Urdu usually does not distinguish them:

- س، ص، ث are usually typed as `s`.
- ز، ذ، ض، ظ are usually typed as `z`.
- ت، ط are usually typed as `t`.
- ہ، ح are often typed as `h`.
- ق may be written as `q` or `k`, depending on the word and the writer.

Do not add specialist symbols to mark these distinctions in casual chat.

### Respect and familiarity

The choice between `tu`, `tum` and `aap` matters:

- `tu` is very familiar and can sound intimate, casual or disrespectful depending on the relationship and tone.
- `tum` is familiar and informal.
- `aap` is respectful or polite.

Examples:

- `Tum kahan ho?`
- `Aap kaise hain?`
- `Tu kya kar raha hai?`

For polite conversation, default to `aap`.

## 7. Common verb forms

Urdu verbs change according to the speaker's gender, the person being addressed, number and level of respect. In casual Roman Urdu, people generally type the spoken form.

### ہونا — to be

| Urdu | Casual Roman Urdu |
|---|---|
| میں ہوں | `main hu` / `main hoon` |
| تم ہو | `tum ho` |
| آپ ہیں | `aap hain` |
| وہ ہے | `wo hai` |
| ہم ہیں | `hum hain` |
| وہ ہیں | `wo hain` |

`hu`, `hun` and `hoon` are all seen for ہوں. `hu` is a compact chat form; `hoon` makes the long vowel clearer.

### کرنا — to do

| Urdu | Casual Roman Urdu |
|---|---|
| میں کرتا ہوں | `main karta hu` |
| میں کرتی ہوں | `main karti hu` |
| تم کرتے ہو | `tum karte ho` |
| تم کرتی ہو | `tum karti ho` |
| آپ کرتے ہیں | `aap karte hain` |
| وہ کرتا ہے | `wo karta hai` |
| وہ کرتی ہے | `wo karti hai` |
| ہم کرتے ہیں | `hum karte hain` |

### جانا — to go

- `main ja raha hu`
- `main ja rahi hu`
- `tum kahan ja rahe ho?`

### آنا — to come

- `main aa raha hu`
- `main aa rahi hu`

### Everyday verb combinations

- `baat karna`
- `intezar karna`

- `call kar lena`
- `message bhej dena`

## 8. Gender and agreement

Urdu commonly marks gender in verbs and adjectives. Casual Roman Urdu writers usually preserve the spoken difference:

- `Main ja raha hu.` — masculine speaker
- `Main ja rahi hu.` — feminine speaker
- `Tum thak gaye?` — masculine/mixed familiar form
- `Tum thak gayi?` — feminine form
- `Wo bohat acha hai.`
- `Wo bohat achi hai.`

Use the form that matches the intended speaker or person. In short messages, context often supplies information that is not explicitly written.

### `mein` versus `main`

This is a useful distinction to preserve when possible:

- `main` = میں (I)
- `mein` = میں (in)

Examples:

- `Main ghar ja raha hu.` — I am going home.
- `Ghar mein koi nahi hai.` — There is nobody at home.

Many people type both in different ways, and context usually resolves the meaning. Using `main` for I and `mein` for in makes messages easier to read.

Short forms differ widely by person and platform. Use them only when the reader will understand them immediately. For general-purpose writing, `nahi`, `hai`, `hain`, `mujhe` and `kar raha hu` are safer than heavily abbreviated forms.

## 11. Urdu-English mixing

Many Urdu speakers naturally mix English words into Urdu sentences. This is ordinary code-mixing, not a failure to write Urdu.

Examples:

- `Kal office jana hai.`
- `Mujhe ye file send kar dena.`
- `Meeting ke baad baat karte hain.`
- `Maine usko message kiya.`
- `Ye photo bohat achi hai.`
- `Thora wait karo, main check karta hu.`
- `Iska screenshot bhej do.`
- `Aaj ka plan cancel ho gaya.`

### Keep English words in normal English spelling

If the word is normally used in English in that context, keep its English spelling:

- `phone`, not a forced transliteration
- `meeting`, not `miting`
- `download`, not `daunlod`
- `message`, not `mesij`
- `update`, not `apdet`

Keep the Urdu grammar and sentence structure natural while using English vocabulary where speakers normally do.

## 13. Regional and community variation

Roman Urdu is used by people with different accents, dialects, educational backgrounds and language repertoires. Spellings can reflect Pakistani regional speech, Indian Urdu usage, diaspora communities, and influence from Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi, English and other languages.

Examples of variation include:

- `bohat` / `bahut`
- `kaise` / `kaisay` / `kese`
- `nahi` / `nahin` / `nai`
- `acha` / `achha` / `accha`
- `kyun` / `kiun` / `kyu`
- `zindagi` / `zindagee`
- `larka` / `ladka`
- `thora` / `thoda`

Some variants reflect pronunciation; others reflect personal spelling habits or influence from another language. This guide uses broadly recognizable Urdu forms, especially those common in Pakistani Roman Urdu, but it does not treat every other spelling as wrong.

## 15. Punctuation, emphasis and chat style

Casual Roman Urdu uses ordinary phone-chat punctuation:

- Questions: `Aap kahan hain?`
- Excitement: `Wah, kya baat hai!`
- Pauses: `Haan, main aa raha hu.`
- Repeated letters for emphasis: `achaaaa`, `nahi yaarrr`, `kyaaaa?`
- Repeated punctuation: `Kya hua??`

Examples:

- `Arey yaar, aisa mat karo.`
- `Haan haan, samajh gaya.`
- `Kyaaaaa! Sach mein?`
- `Theek hai, phir milte hain.`

Use emphasis naturally. Repeated letters are fine in friendly chats, but avoid them in clear instructions or professional messages.

## 16. Common mistakes to avoid

These are practical readability tips, not strict language laws.

1. **Do not use formal transliteration in casual messages.** Write `khush`, not a form with scholarly diacritics.
2. **Do not force every English word into Roman Urdu phonetics.** Use `phone`, `meeting`, `file` and `update`.
3. **Avoid too many abbreviations in one sentence.** `Mujhe nahi pata` is easier to read than `mjhy nhi pta`.
4. **Keep common words recognizable.** Prefer `kaise`, `mujhe`, `bohat`, `acha` and `zindagi` for a broad audience.
5. **Do not assume one spelling is the only correct one.** `nahi`, `nahin` and `nai` are all encountered.
6. **Distinguish `main` and `mein` when possible.** It can prevent ambiguity.
7. **Use `aap` when you need to sound respectful.** `Tum` and `tu` have different levels of familiarity.
8. **Do not randomly switch spellings.** Choose a familiar form and use it consistently within a message.
9. **Remember that some words are ambiguous.** Context matters, especially where Roman Urdu and English spellings overlap.

## 17. Recommended casual house style

For consistent, natural everyday Roman Urdu, use these defaults:

- Use lowercase for ordinary messages.
- Use plain English letters; no diacritics or specialist transliteration marks.
- Prefer `nahi`, `bohat`, `acha`, `kaise`, `kyun`, `kya`, `mujhe`, `zindagi`.
- Use `aap` for respectful address, `tum` for familiar address, and `tu` only when the relationship and tone make it appropriate.
- Use `aa`, `ee` and `oo` when they make a word easier to recognize: `aap`, `theek`, `door`.
- Use familiar combinations such as `sh`, `ch`, `kh`, `gh`, `ph`, `bh`, `th`, `dh`.
- Keep English words in standard English spelling.
- Prefer `main` for I and `mein` for in when possible.
- Use shortened chat forms only when the audience will understand them.
- Accept common regional and personal variants; do not treat casual spelling as a strict standardized system.

## 19. Casual Roman Urdu vs formal romanization

Formal romanization systems are designed to represent Urdu script consistently, often preserving distinctions that everyday speakers do not mark. Some systems use diacritics and special characters so that a Roman form can be converted back into Urdu script more precisely.

Casual Roman Urdu has a different purpose: speed, familiarity and readability.

| Formal romanization | Everyday Roman Urdu |
|---|---|
| Uses diacritics and specialist symbols | Uses ordinary English letters |
| Tries to preserve script distinctions | Often merges sounds that context can clarify |
| Aims for consistency and reversibility | Aims for speed and readability |
| Usually avoids chat abbreviations | May use `nhi`, `h`, `rha` |
| Usually avoids English mixing | Commonly mixes Urdu and English |

For example, formal systems distinguish letters such as ٹ and ت, and may represent ق, ع and other letters with special marks. In everyday Roman Urdu, writers often simplify these distinctions or omit them.

## 20. What the research says

The recommendations above describe common informal patterns; they do not claim that every Urdu speaker types the same way.

- **Roman Urdu is widely used in messaging:** A 2017 study of 116 university students in Pakistan collected 346,455 text messages. The researchers described Roman Urdu as the most widely adopted texting style in their study context and documented multiple spellings for individual words.
- **Spelling is not standardized:** A 2024 open-access dataset paper explains that Roman Urdu has no agreed spelling rules. It provides 5,244 Roman Urdu words with one to five spelling variants and a collection of 28,090 reviews.
- **Online Urdu often mixes with English:** A 2025 research paper on Roman Urdu information retrieval describes informal spelling, frequent English code-switching and user-dependent phonetic spellings as major features of the language online.
- **Social-media posts show substantial code-mixing:** A 2026 study of 900 posts from YouTube, Facebook and X reports extensive Roman Urdu and Urdu-English mixing in its sample.
- **Formal romanization is a different task:** The BGN/PCGN system is designed for standardized, reversible romanization of Urdu geographical names. It uses distinctions and diacritics that are not needed in ordinary chat.

## Final principle

**Write Urdu the way people naturally speak it, using familiar English letters. Keep it simple, readable and conversational.**

Roman Urdu is flexible. The goal is not perfect transliteration; the goal is for another Urdu speaker to read the message naturally and understand what you mean.
