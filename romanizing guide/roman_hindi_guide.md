# Casual Roman Hindi (Hinglish) Typing Guide

*A practical guide to how Hindi is commonly typed in the Roman/English alphabet in everyday Indian digital conversations.*

## 1. What this guide is for

This guide describes **casual Roman Hindi**: Hindi written with English letters, as people commonly do in WhatsApp chats, DMs, comments, texts and informal social media posts.

Examples:

- क्या हुआ? → `kya hua?`
- तुम कहाँ हो? → `tum kaha ho?`
- मुझे नहीं पता → `mujhe nahi pata`
- अभी आ रहा हूँ → `abhi aa raha hu`

People often call this **Roman Hindi**, **Romanagari**, or **Hinglish**. Strictly speaking, Roman Hindi means Hindi written in the Roman alphabet. Hinglish can also mean Hindi and English mixed together in the same sentence:

- `Kal meeting hai, time pe aa jana.`
- `Mujhe ye design kaafi better laga.`

The goal here is not formal transliteration. It is to produce text that looks natural to everyday Hindi readers.

## 2. The most important rule: write how people actually type

There is no single official spelling system for casual Roman Hindi. The same word can be written in several ways, and people often spell according to pronunciation, habit, typing speed and region.

| Hindi | Common casual spellings |
|---|---|
| नहीं | `nahi`, `nahin`, `nhi` |
| बहुत | `bahut`, `bohot`, `boht`, `bhot` |
| अच्छा | `accha`, `achha`, `acha` |
| प्यार | `pyaar`, `pyar` |
| क्या | `kya` |
| जल्दी | `jaldi`, `jldi` |
| ठीक | `theek`, `thik` |
| मुझे | `mujhe`, `muje` |
| तुम्हें | `tumhe`, `tumhein` |
| ऐसा | `aisa`, `esa` |

For a consistent but natural writing style, this guide recommends the **first or most widely recognizable everyday form** in each group. Alternative spellings are still normal.

## 3. Vowels: keep them simple

Casual Roman Hindi does not need academic vowel marks or diacritics. Use ordinary English letters.

| Sound / Hindi example | Casual spelling |
|---|---|
| अ — अब | `ab` |
| आ — आज | `aaj` |
| इ — इधर | `idhar` |
| ई — ठीक | `theek` |
| उ — उठ | `uth` |
| ऊ — दूर | `door` |
| ए — मेरा | `mera` |
| ऐ — ऐसा | `aisa` |
| ओ — बोल | `bol` |
| औ — और | `aur` |

### Long vowels

People often use doubled vowels when it helps show the sound:

- `aa`: `aaj`, `baat`, `naam`
- `ee`: `theek`, `jeet`
- `oo`: `door`, `sooch`

But doubling is not compulsory. For example, `pyar` and `pyaar` are both common.

### Do not overthink short and long vowels

The point is readability, not representing every sound perfectly. Prefer familiar words such as `kya`, `kaise`, `nahi`, `mujhe`, `tumhara` and `accha`.

### `ye` and `wo`

In everyday messaging, `ye` and `wo` are very common for both demonstrative and pronoun uses:

- `Ye kya hai?`
- `Wo kal aaya tha.`
- `Mujhe ye pasand hai.`

Some people use `vo` instead of `wo`. Both are understandable; this guide uses `wo`.

## 7. Common verb forms

Hindi verb endings change with the speaker, the person being addressed, gender, number and level of respect. Casual Roman typing usually writes the spoken form rather than a formal transliteration.

### होना — to be

| Hindi | Casual Roman Hindi |
|---|---|
| मैं हूँ | `main hu` |
| तुम हो | `tum ho` |
| आप हैं | `aap ho` / `aap hain` |
| वह है | `wo hai` |
| हम हैं | `hum hain` |
| वे हैं | `wo hain` |

In fast messages, `hu`, `hun`, `hoon` and `hu` may all appear for हूँ. `hu` is a simple casual default; `hoon` is also widely understood.

### करना — to do

| Hindi | Casual Roman Hindi |
|---|---|
| मैं करता हूँ | `main karta hu` |
| मैं करती हूँ | `main karti hu` |
| तुम करते हो | `tum karte ho` |
| तुम करती हो | `tum karti ho` |
| आप करते हैं | `aap karte hain` |
| वह करता है | `wo karta hai` |
| वह करती है | `wo karti hai` |
| हम करते हैं | `hum karte hain` |

### जाना — to go

- `main ja raha hu`
- `main ja rahi hu`
- `tum kaha ja rahe ho?`
- `wo ghar ja raha hai`
- `hum kal jayenge`

### आना — to come

- `main aa raha hu`
- `main aa rahi hu`
- `kab aaoge?`
- `wo abhi aa raha hai`
- `ghar aa jana`

### करना / कर देना / कर लेना

These are not interchangeable in every context, but they are common in everyday messages:

- `Ye kaam kar do.`
- `Main kar lunga.`
- `Tum kar lena.`
- `Usko call kar dena.`

## 8. Gender and agreement in casual typing

Hindi often marks gender in verbs and adjectives. Roman Hindi writers usually preserve the spoken difference when it matters:

- `Main ja raha hu.` — masculine speaker
- `Main ja rahi hu.` — feminine speaker
- `Tum thak gaye?` — masculine/mixed familiar form
- `Tum thak gayi?` — feminine form
- `Wo bahut accha hai.`
- `Wo bahut acchi hai.`

Do not force gender distinctions where the context does not require them. In quick chats, people may omit words or rely on shared context.

People often shorten common words to type faster. These are casual, not formal spellings.

| `rahi` | `rhi` |

Use shortened forms only when the meaning remains obvious. For a guide or a message intended for a broad audience, `nahi`, `hai`, `mujhe` and `kar raha hu` are safer than heavily abbreviated forms such as `nhi`, `h`, `mje` and `kr rha hu`.

## 11. Hindi-English mixing (Hinglish)

A lot of everyday Indian digital communication naturally mixes Hindi grammar with English nouns, verbs and phrases. This is normal and should not be corrected into pure Hindi.

Examples:

- `Kal office jana hai.`
- `Maine usko message kiya.`
- `Ye file download kar lena.`
- `Meeting ke baad call karna.`
- `Mujhe ye idea pasand aaya.`
- `Aaj ka plan cancel ho gaya.`
- `Iska screenshot bhej do.`
- `Thoda wait karo, main check karta hu.`

### Keep English words in English

If a word is normally used in English in that context, write it in English:

- `phone`, not a forced Hindi transliteration
- `meeting`, not `miting`
- `download`, not `daunlod`
- `message`, not `mesij`
- `update`, not `apdet`

The Hindi parts can stay Roman Hindi while English words retain standard English spelling.

## 13. Regional and pronunciation differences

India is linguistically diverse. Not everyone who writes Hindi online speaks the same variety, and Roman spellings can reflect regional pronunciation, family habits, local vocabulary or Urdu-influenced speech.

Examples of variation:

- `hai` / `he`
- `nahi` / `nahin` / `nai`
- `bahut` / `bohot`
- `accha` / `achha` / `acha`
- `zyada` / `jada`
- `kyun` / `kyu`
- `yaha` / `yahan`
- `waha` / `wahan`
- `ladka` / `larka` (regional or Urdu-influenced preference)

These are not always simple spelling errors. Some reflect real pronunciation and language background. This guide uses broadly recognizable North Indian/Hindi-belt conversational forms as defaults, while allowing common alternatives.

## 15. Punctuation, emphasis and chat style

Casual Roman Hindi generally follows ordinary phone-chat punctuation:

- Use `?` for questions: `Tum kaha ho?`
- Use `!` for excitement: `Arey wah!`
- Use commas when they help: `Haan, main aa raha hu.`
- Full stops are optional in short chats.
- Repeated letters can show emotion: `acchaaaa`, `nahi yaarrr`, `kyaaa?`
- Repeated punctuation is common but should not be overused: `Kya hua??`

Examples:

- `Arey yaar, aisa mat karo.`
- `Haan haan, samajh gaya.`
- `Kyaaaaa! Sach me?`
- `Theek hai, milte hain.`

## 16. Common mistakes to avoid

These are not strict grammar rules; they are practical readability tips.

1. **Do not use formal transliteration in casual chat.** Write `kya`, not `kyā`.
2. **Do not force every English word into Hindi spelling.** Use `phone`, `meeting`, `file`, `update`.
3. **Avoid too many abbreviations in one sentence.** `Mujhe nahi pata` is easier to read than `mje nhi pta`.
4. **Keep common words recognizable.** Prefer `kaise`, `mujhe`, `bahut`, `accha` over unusual personal spellings when writing for others.
5. **Do not assume one spelling is the only correct one.** `nahi`, `nahin` and `nhi` are all seen online.
6. **Do not mix spellings randomly.** Choose a familiar form and use it consistently within the same message.
7. **Do not confuse Hindi and English homographs.** Words such as `me`, `is`, `to` can be Hindi words or English words; the sentence context makes the meaning clear.

## 17. Recommended casual house style

For consistent everyday Roman Hindi, use these defaults:

- Use lowercase for ordinary messages.
- Use plain English letters; no diacritics.
- Prefer `nahi`, `hai`, `hain`, `hu`, `kya`, `kyun`, `kaise`.
- Prefer `accha`, `bahut`, `mujhe`, `tumhe`, `aapko`.
- Use `aa`, `ee` and `oo` when they make a word easier to recognize: `aaj`, `theek`, `door`.
- Use `sh`, `ch`, `kh`, `gh`, `th`, `dh`, `bh` in familiar spellings.
- Keep English words in normal English spelling.
- Preserve natural Hindi word order, even when English words are mixed in.
- Use shortened chat forms only when the audience will understand them.
- Accept common regional and personal variants; do not treat casual spelling as a strict standardized system.

## 19. Casual Roman Hindi vs formal transliteration

This guide is about everyday typing, not a formal system for representing every Devanagari letter precisely.

| Formal / technical approach | Everyday chat approach |
|---|---|
| Uses diacritics or specialist symbols | Uses ordinary English letters |
| Tries to preserve script distinctions | Often merges sounds that context can clarify |
| Aims for consistency and reversibility | Aims for speed and readability |
| Avoids informal abbreviations | May use `nhi`, `h`, `rha` |
| Usually avoids English mixing | Commonly mixes Hindi and English |

For example, a formal scheme may distinguish sounds that casual writers both type as `t` or `d`. Everyday users generally do not need that distinction in ordinary messages.

## 20. What the research says

The recommendations above are based on observed Romanized Hindi and Hinglish use, not on a claim that all Indians type identically.

- **No single standard spelling:** A 2026 paper on natural Hindi typing notes that forms such as `nahi`, `nahin` and `nai` coexist, and that users often lengthen or drop vowels or double consonants. It argues that useful transliteration should reflect attested user spellings rather than impose a scholarly system.
- **Roman script is common online:** The same paper reports earlier corpus findings in which Roman-script Hindi-English social posts substantially outnumbered Devanagari-script posts in the studied dataset. This is evidence about particular online corpora, not a claim about every Indian or every platform.
- **Hinglish is genuinely mixed:** The 2024 *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* study describes Hinglish as a growing conversational practice online and offline, and gives examples of Hindi grammar combined with English words.
- **Real social-media text is variable:** The PHINC research corpus contains 13,738 Hindi-English code-mixed sentences. Its authors identify spelling variation, informal grammar, language-identification ambiguity and expressive repeated letters as challenges in user-generated text.
- **Romanagari is used in messaging:** Research on WhatsApp communication among Indian university students describes Romanagari as a common informal practice and examines how users move between Roman Hindi and English.

## Final principle

**Write Hindi the way people naturally say it, using the English alphabet. Keep it familiar, simple and readable.**

Casual Roman Hindi is flexible. The goal is not perfect transliteration; the goal is for another Hindi speaker to read the message instantly and understand exactly what you mean.
